# Hook patterns

The first line does roughly 80% of the work, because it is the only line most readers
ever see. Everything here is about that line.

## The taxonomy

Classify every hook into exactly one type. If it fits none, that is the diagnosis: an
unclassifiable opening is almost always preamble.

| Type | Shape | Works when | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tension** | Two things that should not both be true | The contradiction is real and specific | The tension is manufactured or resolved in the same line |
| **Number** | A concrete figure in the first five words | The number is unusual, checkable and yours | The number is round, borrowed or decorative |
| **Contrarian** | Names a common belief and rejects it | You can actually defend the position | It is contrarian about something nobody believes |
| **Identity-callout** | Names the reader's situation precisely | The reader recognises themselves in it | The group is so broad it names nobody |
| **Story cold-open** | Drops into a scene mid-action | The scene has stakes and a person in it | It starts before the interesting part |
| **Question** | Asks something the reader is already asking | The question is one they have actually asked | It is rhetorical, or answerable with "no" |

Question hooks are the weakest type on average and the most common. A yes/no question is
a permission slip to scroll. If a draft opens with one, that alone is usually worth
flagging.

## The four failure modes

Check every hook against all four. Name the ones present and quote the offending words.

### 1. Vague benefit

The line promises improvement without naming anything concrete.

> "Here are some tips to help you grow your business."

Nothing here is checkable, so nothing is interesting. The test: could this sentence
appear, unchanged, above ten thousand different posts? If yes, it is a vague benefit.

Fix by replacing the abstraction with the most specific true detail in the draft. If the
draft contains no specific detail, the problem is the post, not the hook.

### 2. Buried subject

The real subject appears after a dozen words of scaffolding.

> "After a lot of reflection over the past few months, and several conversations with
> people I respect, I've come to realise that our approach to hiring was wrong."

The subject — hiring was wrong — arrives at word 26, well past the fold on most
platforms. Move it to word one.

The test: find the word the post is actually about and count its position. Past word
twelve, it is buried.

### 3. Throat-clearing preamble

The opening is the author warming up rather than the post starting.

> "I've been thinking about this for a while and wanted to share some thoughts."
> "Long post, sorry."
> "Hi everyone, hope you're all having a good week."
> "So today I wanted to talk about..."

None of these carry information. They are the sound of someone deciding to speak. Delete
them entirely — in almost every case, the second sentence is the real first sentence.
Say that directly: "your hook is your second line; cut the first."

### 4. False curiosity

The line withholds something to force a click, and the post does not repay it.

> "The one thing nobody tells you about pricing."
> "I made a decision last year that changed everything."

Curiosity is only legitimate when the post closes the gap it opens, and closes it well.
The test: does the body deliver something that justifies the withholding? If the answer
is a truism, the hook has spent trust it cannot repay, and readers who feel baited
punish the next post too.

Note the distinction from a legitimate tension hook. Tension states both halves of a
contradiction. False curiosity states neither and points at a box.

## Worked rewrites

Each of these uses only facts already present in the original. Never invent a figure to
strengthen a hook.

**Before (vague benefit, question type):**
> "Want to know how to write better subject lines? Here's what I've learned."

**After (number):**
> "We rewrote 40 subject lines last quarter. Three changes accounted for every gain."

**After (contrarian):**
> "Personalising subject lines with a first name did nothing for us. Specificity did."

---

**Before (throat-clearing, buried subject):**
> "I wanted to take a moment to share something I've been reflecting on recently. Over
> the last year our team has grown from 4 to 11, and it hasn't been easy."

**After (tension):**
> "We grew from 4 people to 11 in a year, and shipped less than we did at 4."

**After (story cold-open):**
> "At 11 people, our standup took 40 minutes. At 4, we didn't have one."

---

**Before (false curiosity):**
> "There's one mistake almost every founder makes with their first hire. I made it too."

**After (identity-callout):**
> "If your first hire is a junior version of you, you have bought yourself more of your
> own weaknesses."

---

**Before (question, rhetorical):**
> "Are you struggling to get engagement on LinkedIn?"

**After (number):**
> "My last 20 posts averaged 900 impressions. Two got 14,000. Both broke the same rule."

## Scoring notes

When applying the 0-2 rubric in SKILL.md:

- **Specificity** rewards proper nouns, figures, timescales and named consequences.
  Adjectives are not specificity.
- **Subject position** is mechanical. Count the words.
- **Stakes** asks what is at risk. A post where nothing could have gone wrong scores 0
  regardless of how well written it is.
- **Curiosity honesty** is scored against the body, not the hook. You must read the whole
  post to score this dimension.
- **Reader fit** scores 2 only when a specific reader would think "that is me". Broad
  appeal scores low here on purpose; broad hooks underperform.

A hook can be well written and score 4. Craft and function are different axes, and the
score measures function. Say so if the user pushes back on a low score for a line they
are fond of.
