# Sales and Outreach

You could be the world's best editor, but if you don't have clients, you make $0. Most
people spend months "perfecting" their craft or building a website, which generates zero
revenue. Sales and Outreach is the only skill on the list that directly generates cash
flow. It allows you to skip the "waiting to be discovered" phase and go straight to
pitching.

This skill turns that premise into a working system: pick who to pitch, find them, write
a message they answer, follow up until it converts, and close without underpricing.

## The one rule

**Pitching beats polishing.** Any time the user proposes to improve the website, redo the
portfolio, or take another course before sending outreach, redirect them: those activities
feel like progress and generate no revenue. The deliverable of every session with this
skill is messages sent, or ready to send — never assets that wait to be discovered.

## Sequence

### 1. Define the offer in one sentence

Before any pitch, the user must be able to say: **who** they help, **what outcome** they
deliver, and **what it costs the client to not have it**. "I edit videos" is a commodity;
"I cut podcast episodes into short clips that grow your channel while you sleep" is an
offer. If the user cannot fill this in, build it with them first — a strong message for a
vague offer converts nothing.

### 2. Pick a niche narrow enough to research

A pitch to "businesses" is a pitch to no one. Narrow until the user can name twenty real
prospects: not "podcasters" but "B2B podcasts with 50–500 episodes and no clips channel".
The test of a good niche is that the same three pain points show up in every prospect, so
one message template genuinely fits all of them.

### 3. Build the prospect list

Twenty named prospects minimum before the first message goes out. For each: the person
(not the info@ address), where they publish, one specific observation about their current
output, and the trigger that makes them worth pitching now (new launch, visible gap,
recent complaint, growth spurt). A list without per-prospect observations produces
template spam, and template spam is why cold outreach has a bad name.

### 4. Write the pitch

Structure every cold message in four parts, in order:

| Part | Job | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opener | Prove you actually looked at their work — one specific observation | 1 sentence |
| Problem | Name the gap you noticed and what it costs them | 1–2 sentences |
| Offer | What you would do about it, with proof if you have it | 1–2 sentences |
| Ask | One small, easy yes — not "hire me" | 1 sentence |

Rules that are not negotiable: under 150 words; no "I hope this finds you well"; no
paragraph about the sender's background; the ask is a conversation or a small free sample,
never the full engagement; and the message must contain at least one detail that could
only apply to this prospect. If the message could be sent to anyone on the list unchanged,
it is not ready.

### 5. Send in batches and follow up

Volume with tracking beats perfection without it. Ten personalized messages a day beats
fifty templated ones. Follow up on silence at day 3, day 7, and day 14 — each follow-up
adds something new (a relevant idea, a sample, a result for a similar client), never
"just bumping this". Three follow-ups, then move on; the list is long and the time is
better spent on the next twenty prospects. Track every send: prospect, date, message
variant, reply or silence. After forty sends, the data says which opener and which offer
convert — iterate on that, not on instinct.

### 6. Handle the reply

- **Interested** — move to a call or a scoped first project within two messages. Momentum
  decays fast; a warm reply left for three days is a cold one.
- **"Not right now"** — ask permission to check back in a stated month, log it, and
  actually check back. These convert later at a higher rate than fresh cold outreach.
- **Price objection** — do not discount first. Shrink the scope instead: a smaller paid
  pilot proves the value at a price they'll say yes to, and prices the real engagement.
- **Silence after interest** — one direct question: "Should I close this out?" It revives
  more deals than another value paragraph.

### 7. Close and price

First projects are priced to win the relationship, not the invoice — but never free, and
never open-ended. A scoped pilot with a fixed price, a fixed deliverable and a fixed date
converts a stranger into a client. On delivery, ask for two things in the same message:
the ongoing engagement, and one referral to someone with the same problem. A referral from
a paying client outperforms any cold message the user will ever write.

## Presentation

Output ready-to-send text, not advice about text. When the user asks for a pitch, write
the pitch — personalized to a real prospect if one is named, with the placeholders clearly
marked if not. When reviewing their draft, quote the weak lines, say why they fail, and
rewrite them. End every session with the next concrete send: who, what message, when.

## Limits of the method

- **It cannot verify prospect details.** Names, publications and observations the user
  supplies are trusted; anything generated should be flagged for the user to verify before
  sending — a wrong detail in a personalized pitch is worse than no pitch.
- **Reply rates are not promisable.** Cold outreach converts on volume and iteration;
  any specific rate quoted in advance is invented.
- **Deliverability is out of scope.** Domain warm-up, spam filters and sending
  infrastructure matter at volume and are not covered here.
- **It does not replace the craft.** Outreach gets the client; the work keeps them. A
  pipeline full of clients who churn after one project is a pitching success and a
  business failure.
