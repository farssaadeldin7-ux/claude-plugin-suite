# The meeting deck

A first sales meeting is twenty to thirty minutes of a stranger's attention. The deck
that earns it is not a company presentation — no history slide, no logo wall, no product
tour. It is the prospect's own problem played back to them, priced in their numbers,
with one proposed change and one ask. If the deck would work for a different prospect
unchanged, it is a brochure, and the find-and-replace test from the pitch applies to
slides exactly as it does to emails.

## The skeleton

Five spine slides, in this order, always. **Eight slides maximum** including the title
and any supporting slide — at slide nine the meeting is being lectured, not held.

| Slide | Job | The content comes from | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The problem, as they stated it | Prove the seller listened | The prospect's reply, call notes, their job posting or public comments — quoted | Paraphrasing their words into the product's vocabulary |
| 2. What it costs them | Make the problem worth thirty minutes | Their numbers: figures they gave, published, or confirmed | An invented industry statistic standing in for their number |
| 3. The change | Describe their operation after the fix, not the product | The offer, mapped onto the workflow they described | A feature list |
| 4. Proof | Show the change has happened before | One comparable, sourceable result the seller can name and date | A vague "companies like yours" claim |
| 5. The ask | Leave with a decision to make | The scoped pilot: fixed price, fixed deliverable, fixed date | "Any questions?" as the closing slide |

## Per-slide rules

- **One claim per slide.** A slide making two points is two slides, or one point too
  many. The test: the slide's job can be said in one sentence, and everything on it
  serves that sentence.
- **The prospect's own words, quoted, where known.** Their phrasing from the reply, the
  call, or their public comments goes on the slide in quotation marks, attributed. Their
  words on slide 1 do more than any headline the seller writes.
- **No metric on a slide the seller cannot source.** Every number must have a nameable
  source the seller could state aloud if asked — and the answer to "where is that from?"
  will be asked. A number that survives only until that question loses the room and the
  deal.
- **Every number comes from the account research or from the prospect — never
  invented.** No projected savings, no "typical results", no industry average the seller
  has not actually read. If slide 2 has no real numbers, present the cost as a question
  to the prospect ("what does a lost week of this cost you?") rather than a figure. A
  question mark is honest; a made-up figure is the fastest way to end the relationship.

## Sourcing

Where the account was researched with the `sales-enablement-assistant` skill, its
research brief supplies slides 1 and 2 directly: the trigger with its source and date is
slide 1's evidence, and the buyer's stated priority is the language slide 3 must be
written in. The same honesty rule from that skill's compliance pass binds the deck:
metrics, case-study numbers and customer counts the user has not supplied are never
added, and a request for one is declined with the honest version offered instead.

## When there is no deck

If the prospect has not yet stated a problem, there is nothing to play back, and a deck
built anyway becomes the generic company presentation this method exists to avoid. Hold
a discovery conversation without slides instead, and build the deck for the second
meeting from what it surfaces.
