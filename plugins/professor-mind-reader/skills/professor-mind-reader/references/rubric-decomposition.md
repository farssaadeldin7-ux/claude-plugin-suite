# Rubric decomposition

A criterion is three things wearing one coat: a **weight**, an **observable** the marker
can point at, and a **verb** that fixes the cognitive level. Separate them before you
read a single word of the draft.

## The verb ladder

Ranked lowest to highest. The rung the criterion names is the rung the draft must reach.

| Level | Verbs used in rubrics | What the marker must be able to underline | The one-level-down failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Describe | describe, outline, identify, state, summarise, define | Accurate account of what the thing is | Listing without accuracy or relevance |
| 2 Explain | explain, illustrate, demonstrate, account for, clarify | A mechanism or cause: X happens **because** Y | Describing the thing and stopping |
| 3 Apply | apply, use, calculate, implement, model | The concept operating on the specific case in front of it | Explaining the concept in the abstract |
| 4 Analyse | analyse, examine, investigate, distinguish, deconstruct | The thing broken into parts, with the relationship between parts named | Applying a framework and not saying what it reveals |
| 5 Compare | compare, contrast, relate, differentiate | An explicit stated basis of comparison, and both sides on it | Two descriptions side by side with no bridge |
| 6 Evaluate | evaluate, critique, assess, justify, argue, appraise, judge | A verdict, with the criteria used to reach it, and the strongest objection answered | Analysing thoroughly and never committing |
| 7 Synthesise | synthesise, integrate, formulate, design, propose, theorise | Something new built from parts that were separate — a framework, position or model | Evaluating each source in turn without joining them |

### The demotion test

For each criterion, take the strongest sentence the draft offers and ask which rung it
sits on. If it is one below, that criterion loses roughly a band. **This is the single
most common cause of a competent essay landing in the middle.** It reads fluently, is
factually fine, and answers a lower question than the one asked.

Signals of a demoted answer:

- "This shows that..." followed by restating the evidence rather than interpreting it
- A source summarised in three sentences with no sentence about what it is worth
- A framework named and applied, with no sentence on what the application revealed
- A conclusion that recaps the sections rather than answering the question
- Hedged non-commitment: "both views have merit" with no basis for preferring one

### Promotion moves

| From | To | The move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Describe | Explain | Add "because" and a mechanism |
| Explain | Apply | Attach it to the case, with specifics from the case |
| Apply | Analyse | Name what the application revealed that was not obvious |
| Analyse | Evaluate | State the criteria for judgement, then judge |
| Evaluate | Synthesise | Reconcile two positions into one that is yours |

## Weight-to-effort mapping

```
words per criterion (target) = total words x criterion weight
marks per 100 words          = criterion weight / (total words / 100)
investment ratio             = actual section words / target words
```

| Investment ratio | Verdict | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above 1.5 | Over-invested | Cut. The marks are not there. |
| 1.5 to 1.2 | Slightly heavy | Acceptable if the criterion is high-verb |
| 1.2 to 0.8 | On budget | Leave it |
| 0.8 to 0.5 | Thin | Expand if the criterion is unmet |
| Below 0.5 | Under-invested | Usually the biggest available gain |

Two standing rules:

- A criterion at or above 20% that is **unmet** outranks every other fix, regardless of
  effort.
- A criterion at or below 10% that is already **met** is finished. Additional polish
  there converts into no marks.

High-verb criteria justify a ratio above 1.0: evaluation costs more words per mark than
description, because it needs the evidence, the judgement and the objection.

## Worked decomposition

**Brief:** "Critically evaluate the effectiveness of remote working policies on
organisational productivity. 2,500 words."

**Rubric as printed:**

| # | Criterion | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Knowledge and understanding of relevant theory | 20% |
| B | Critical evaluation of evidence | 40% |
| C | Application to organisational context | 25% |
| D | Structure, clarity and referencing | 15% |

**Decomposed:**

| # | Verb level | Observable the marker points at | Target words | Marks per 100 words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 2 Explain | Two or three named theories, each with its mechanism stated, not just its name | 500 | 0.8 |
| B | 6 Evaluate | At least three places where the draft weighs one study against another and says which is more persuasive and why; the strongest counter-position answered | 1,000 | 1.6 |
| C | 3 Apply | Named organisation or sector, with its specifics driving the argument rather than illustrating it | 625 | 1.0 |
| D | — | Signposted sections, consistent citation style, no orphan references | 375 | 0.6 |

**What this immediately tells you:**

- Criterion B is worth twice criterion A and demands four rungs higher. A draft that
  spends its length carefully explaining theory is optimising the cheap criterion.
- The whole essay turns on B. If the draft has no sentence of the form "X is more
  persuasive than Y because...", B is unmet and 40% of the mark is uncollected.
- Criterion D is 15% and largely mechanical. It should be finished in one editing pass,
  not agonised over.
- C fails silently when the organisation appears only as an example. The test is whether
  removing the organisation would change the argument. If it would not, C is unmet.

**Typical draft, audited:** 900 words on A, 700 on B, 500 on C, 400 on D. Ratios 1.8 /
0.7 / 0.8 / 1.07. The diagnosis writes itself: 400 words move from A to B, and B needs
promotion from rung 4 to rung 6, not merely more length.
